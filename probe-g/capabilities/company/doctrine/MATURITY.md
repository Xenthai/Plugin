# Maturity doctrine — is the engagement building capability, or dependency?

Read this when scoping an engagement, at every quarterly report, and before any renewal
conversation.

It adapts a five-level adoption model and a six-month phase framework published by Anthropic in
April 2026. **The adaptation is the important part**, and it is not cosmetic: the published model
describes *a company deploying to itself*, where its own staff become the builders. This practice
deploys *to a client*. Copying the model without that translation would describe an engagement that
looks successful and leaves nothing behind.

---

## 1. The five levels, as they apply to a client

| Level | What the client can do | The published example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **0** | Ask a question and get an answer. Chat, essentially | *"Summarize what was decided in #project-atlas this week."* |
| **1** | Produce something from their own files and systems — a deck, an email, a spreadsheet, a diagram | *"Here's the deal folder. Draft an investment memo."* |
| **2** | **Turn a repeated task into a skill.** One file runs the same task the same way every time | A variance-analysis skill *"that knows your tables, your thresholds, and your CFO's preferred format"* |
| **3** | Bundle skills, build their own, and **schedule them** to run unattended | *"A morning briefing that fires at 7:30am, pulling calendar, pipeline, and overnight Slack into one page"* |
| **4** | A curated, admin-provisioned bundle per department — sales, legal, finance, marketing each run their own | A legal plugin: *"intake triage, regulatory monitoring, and exec updates, encoded once and shared with the whole team"* |

Two statements from the source that set the ambition correctly, and both are worth repeating to a
client in the first meeting:

> **Level 0 is where almost everyone is today, and it's a perfectly fine place to be on day one.**

> **The job of a deployment is to get every user one level higher than they are now and to make sure
> the path upward is paved; nobody needs to reach Level 4 in the first month.**

A maturity model whose stated goal is **one level of movement** is a model a client can believe. One
that implies they should reach the top is a sales instrument, and they have seen those.

---

## 2. The translation this practice has to make

The published framework runs on **champions**: the client's own early adopters who write their own
skills, and whose authored skills are then provisioned org-wide. Its stated leading indicator says
so plainly:

> **The signal that a pilot is working isn't just hours saved. It's champions starting to write
> their own skills.** … **Track how many champion-authored skills exist at the end of the pilot;
> it's the leading indicator for the scale phase.**

**A consulting engagement can quietly invert that.** Xenth AI does the building, so the client's
level never moves — they stay at Level 1 consuming deliverables while the practice does the Level 2,
3 and 4 work on their behalf. The engagement can look excellent by every other measure and still
leave the client exactly where it found them.

So this doctrine adds one measure the published model does not need:

> **Whose level moved?** If only the practice's did, the engagement produced dependency, not
> capability — and that is a finding for the annual report, not a secret.

This is the same test the handover doctrine applies to a single automation, at the scale of a whole
engagement: **a client who cannot switch it off does not own it, and a client who cannot run it
without us does not own the capability either.**

That does not make Level 4 the goal for every client. Some deliberately buy a permanent service and
that is a legitimate arrangement — **but it must be a stated arrangement rather than a drift**, and
the client should hear it named at the start rather than discover it at renewal.

## 3. The six-month shape

Reproduced with its published targets, then what this practice adds to each phase.

| Phase | When | Target | The published actions | What this practice adds |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Evaluate** | Month 1 | Champions reach Level 1 | Security review · identify 2–3 champion teams · install pre-built plugins · connect 1–2 core systems | **The mapping engagement is this phase.** Capture the before, sign the starting numbers, and name which two systems get connected |
| **Pilot** | Months 2–3 | Champions reach Levels 2–3 | Champions run real workflows · weekly check-ins · measure against defined criteria · demo wins to adjacent teams | Every workflow enters at **rung 1 of the autonomy ladder**, and the quarterly report is the "measure against defined criteria" |
| **Scale** | Months 4–6 | Department reaches Level 4 | Admin-provisioned marketplace · encode pilot learnings as org-wide skills · onboard the next wave | The skills built during the pilot are **the client's**, not ours. Handover is a deliverable of this phase, not an afterthought |

> **Each phase has a different audience, motion, and success signal.**

And the pattern that governs the whole shape:

> **The pattern is bottom-up discovery, top-down scale.** Let teams experiment and find what works
> for their function, then take what works and provision it org-wide.

**Do not prescribe from above in month one.** The published instruction is to let the early adopters
*"surface use cases that matter to their function rather than prescribing from above"* — which is
also why the process-mapping engagement interviews the person who does the work rather than the
person who describes it.

## 4. The cold-start problem decides month one

> **The cold-start problem is real: if someone opens Claude Cowork and doesn't know what to do, they
> close it. If they open it, type `/morning-briefing`, and get something useful in ninety seconds,
> they come back tomorrow.**

Ninety seconds is the budget. It has two consequences for an engagement:

- **Something useful must run in the first session**, before anything is mapped or measured. A
  first session that produces only questions is a first session that ends the pilot.
- **Connected systems are what make Level 1 possible at all.** The source is explicit: without them
  *"users stay stuck at Level 0 chat."* A plugin with no reach into the client's own data is a
  chatbot with extra steps, and the client will correctly say so.

That is why company creation runs `doctor` before intake, and why intake asks for files rather than
opinions: **both exist to make the first useful output arrive early.**

---

## 5. What Level 4 changes, and why it is the argument for the whole engagement

> **A new hire who installs the department's plugin on day one starts at Level 2, not Level 0. They
> get the encoded workflows before they've had time to develop bad habits, and the floor for the
> whole team rises.**

And the sentence that is this practice's entire process-mapping pitch, written by someone else:

> **Tribal knowledge gets encoded and reused rather than walking out the door when someone leaves.**

Note what that implies for pricing and sequencing. The absence of written procedure is already
measured in `BASELINE.md` as the tribal-knowledge count. **Level 4 is that count going down**, and
it is a number the client can check without trusting the practice's judgement.

One more, on scale versus quality:

> **It's a Level 2 asset doing Level 4 work: one well-built skill that quietly became how an entire
> function approaches a recurring problem.**

A single well-built artefact used by a whole function beats a bundle nobody opens. **Level is not a
score to maximise** — it describes what the client can do, and one thing done well at Level 2 is
worth more than four unused bundles at Level 4.

---

## 6. What converts a sceptic

> **The skeptics converted not after a demo but after a couple hours of running real workflows.**

A demo shows what the tool can do. A real workflow on a document the sceptic recognises shows what
it does *here*. Budget the session accordingly: **bring their contract, their quote, their report** —
not an example.

And the shape of the change to promise, from the same client:

> **The human role becomes validation, refinement, and decision-making. Not repetitive rework.**

That sentence is honest and it is sellable, and it is the same posture as the autonomy ladder: the
person keeps the decision and loses the retyping.

## 6b. A client's own estimate of their readiness is the least reliable input available

The artefact rule in §7 exists because self-assessment fails, and it fails in a specific, predictable
shape worth naming before a client uses it on you.

Asked to rate their data foundations for AI, executives distribute roughly: 22% very ready, 53%
somewhat ready, 17% somewhat unready, 8% very unready — a poll of 275+ executives in *Data
strategies for AI leaders*, MIT Technology Review Insights, May 2024. **That paper is sponsored by
Snowflake**, discloses about three of AAPOR's eleven minimal items, and reports its own sample size
as "275+", which is not a number. Use the shape, never the decimals, and name the sponsor if it is
mentioned aloud.

The shape is what matters, and two experts in the same paper say the same thing about it from
different directions:

- **"Somewhat ready" is where three-quarters of the honest answers land, and it is not a level.** It
  is the answer a person gives when they can integrate data across the business and also know the
  underlying data is not clean. Arnab Chakraborty of Accenture puts the consequence plainly: on
  accuracy and data quality, *50% ready is not good enough*, because the threshold for trust is
  higher than the midpoint.
- **"Somewhat ready" is rarely "almost ready".** Neil Ward-Dutton of IDC Europe reports a bell curve
  in practice — a front end that has already invested in governance and quality, and *"a bunch of
  people still using, basically, Excel for everything"* — with the middle much closer to the Excel
  end than its self-rating implies.

Both are expert opinion rather than data, and neither is treated here as evidence. They are useful
because they predict the same failure the level model already assumes: a client will place
themselves one level above the artefact they can produce.

### The counter-intuitive finding, and why it matters at renewal

The companies rating themselves **very ready face MORE data-quality problems, not fewer** — better
access to their information revealed shortcomings that were always there.

This is the single most useful thing in that paper, because it inverts what a client expects from a
successful engagement and it will happen to yours. Month one produces order; month six produces a
list of defects nobody could see in month one. A client not warned about this reads that list as the
engagement having made things worse.

So warn them at the mapping close, before it happens: **the number of known problems is expected to
rise, and a rising count of known problems is the instrument working.** Then, when it rises, the
warning is what makes the finding credible instead of alarming — and `opportunities` is what surfaces
the list, which is why its output belongs in the quarterly rather than being saved up.

## 7. Record it, or it is an opinion

The client's level and phase belong in `BASELINE.md`'s maturity layer, per department, with a date —
because a level asserted in a meeting is a feeling, and a level recorded with the artefact that
proves it is a measurement.

The evidence for each level is the artefact that could not exist at the level below:

| Claimed level | The artefact that proves it |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Something produced from the client's own files, not from a blank prompt |
| 2 | A skill file in the client's own store, run more than once |
| 3 | A schedule, and a record of it having run unattended |
| 4 | A bundle installed by someone who did not build it |

**No artefact, no level.** And the level that matters at renewal is not the practice's — it is
theirs.
